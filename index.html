Student-led initiative · Punjab, India

When a parent goes to prison, a child's life changes too.

The People's Docket works inside Punjab's jails with incarcerated mothers and fathers, and with their children. We listen, we share practical information, and we try to connect families with the help they are already entitled to.

Why this work

The children of prisoners are easy to overlook

Most conversations about prisons are about the people inside. Their children rarely come up, even though a parent's arrest can change where a child lives, whether they stay in school, and how often they ever see that parent again.

1,867

children living inside Indian prisons

Children under six can stay with their mothers in jail. Many more, of every age, live outside with relatives while a parent is inside.

NCRB, Prison Statistics India 2021

What a child can lose

Our conversations with parents keep returning to the same things: schooling that stops, documents like Aadhaar that were never made, and long gaps between visits because the journey costs too much.

What the law already says

In R.D. Upadhyay v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2006), the Supreme Court said these children have a right to food, care and education, and that a child sent out of jail at six should be able to meet their mother every week. Our work is about helping those rights reach the children they were written for.

How we work

The rules we keep

We work with people in a difficult place, and with children. These are the rules we follow in every visit.

  • Only with permissionWe go only where the Punjab Prisons Department has allowed us, and we follow each jail's rules.
  • Only with consentEvery conversation is voluntary. Anyone can decline a question or stop at any time.
  • No names, no facesWe never publish the names, photographs or identifying details of inmates or their children.
  • Children come firstAny help we arrange for a child goes through their parent's consent and an organisation qualified to give it.
What's next

Where the docket goes from here

Each of these ideas came out of what we heard inside.

Drug-awareness workshops in Punjab's villages

Sessions for school students and young people in the districts most affected by drugs, on what drug use costs your health, your family and your future, and why a case can't simply be fixed through connections.

Suggested to us by inmates themselves.

Connecting children with support

With their parents' consent, linking children of incarcerated parents to NGOs that help with schooling, housing and everyday needs.

Many parents told us their child's schooling had suffered.

Hygiene essentials for women inmates

Raising funds to provide sanitary pads and other basic essentials, and helping with dispensers where they are needed.

A written record of what we learn

Bringing our conversations together into a report on the lives of children of incarcerated parents in Punjab, with every family kept anonymous.
